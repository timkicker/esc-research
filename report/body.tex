\section{Question and answer}

In the Eurovision final 25 to 27 countries compete and every country taking part that year votes. Each vote gives 12, 10 and 8 down to 1 points to ten other countries, never to itself. Up to 2015 a country casts one vote. Since 2016 its jury and its public vote separately and both are published \citep{adamska2025}. We ask how well the winner of the final can be predicted from data that exists the day before. Our canvas named two groups who would want to know: people who trade on prediction markets and the contestants themselves.

The model we chose on the validation finals puts the real winner of 2024, 2025 and 2026 in first, third and third place, where a random order would put it around 13th. Leaving out each of the 18 finals once, the winner lands in its top three nine times. The rest of the scoreboard stays rough: 6.42 places off on average against 8.43 for a random order.

\section{Data}

A row is one directed country pair in one final: country A votes for the entry of country B. Greece gave Cyprus 10 jury points and 12 public points in 2024, so that row has the target 11. Before that final Greece had given Cyprus 10.4 points on average in 27 earlier finals. We predict rows and not entries because the 18 finals from 2008 to 2026 (2020 was cancelled) have only 462 entries, too few for a dozen features, while they give 18\,069 pair rows. Features such as distance or earlier votes also only exist for a pair. Up to 2015 the target is the single vote of a country. From 2016 it is half of jury plus public, so every year has one 0 to 12 scale and the sum per entry is half of its official points. Since 2023 viewers in countries without an entry vote together as ``Rest of the World'', a public vote without a jury that we halve like any other. Half of the pair rows are zero (50.3 percent), because only ten countries score per vote.

\input{tab-sources}

Table~\ref{tab:sources} lists the sources. Per pair we use the logarithm of the distance, a shared border, two measures of shared language, three of colonial ties plus the mean points and number of earlier meetings of this directed pair in all finals since 1957. Per entry we use the running order, the age and sex of solo artists as well as mood, theme and repetition of the lyrics. The lyrics were labelled by a language model through an OpenAI compatible interface at temperature 0, with fixed answer categories. The configured model is gpt-oss-120b on the e-INFRA CZ service, the output file does not record which model ran.

Google Trends gives the search interest in each voting country for each entry, for web search and YouTube, over the 30 days up to the day before the final. Trends does not report search volumes. It scales the country with the most interest to 100 and shares that scale between all terms of one query, so every entry is queried alone \citep{googletrends}. A value therefore says how much a country searched for an entry compared with the country that searched most. A teammate suspected that time zones could pull the final itself into the window. The query uses UTC and ends the day before, the final starts at about 19:00 UTC, so even a shifted day boundary ends the window at least seven hours before the show. We checked the stored windows for 2009, 2015, 2017 and 2024 to 2026 and the dates of all 17 finals with search data (2010 has none). The same script queried the years before 2016. The search data are weak before 2016: both values are zero in 98.7 percent of the rows in 2008, 72.6 percent in 2016 and 38.0 percent in 2024. 2010 has neither searches nor lyrics. Age and sex are missing for a third of the entries, mostly bands.

The pipeline is written in Python with pandas and stores its tables as Parquet. One command (\texttt{uv run esc all}) downloads, cleans and joins all sources on Linux and Windows. Network access happens only in the download step, every response is cached and every source is listed in \texttt{sources.yml}, with a checksum wherever the source has a fixed release. The tests run offline. The pipeline builds its table from 2008 on. A short notebook adds age and sex and saves it as one CSV file, so the whole group could work without running the pipeline. The prediction notebook reads this file. Rebuilt from the pipeline on 17 October, it matched the file used here in all 27\,918 rows. A second notebook of the group plots what winners have in common (age, sex, running order, language, lyrics, searches) and fed the slides. The only people in the data are the artists, who are public figures.

\section{Method}

Nothing goes into the model that is measured after the vote. That rules out three things. The semi-final points are published after the final. The points other countries gave the same entry are the result itself. YouTube view counts are only available as today's count, which includes every view the winner got for winning. \citet{adamska2025} point out this leak for their own view counts.

The finals are split by year and never at random, since the rows of one final depend on each other: training 2008 to 2019, validation 2021 to 2023, test 2024 to 2026. Missing values are filled with the median of the training years. We fit linear regression and lasso on standardised features (the lasso penalty by cross validation over groups of years), a random forest with 24 settings and gradient boosting with 8, all with scikit-learn \citep{pedregosa2011}. Settings and model are chosen by the average place of the real winners of the validation finals. A tie goes to the higher R$^2$ on the validation rows. Gradient boosting without the artist and lyric features and a forest without them both found all three winners. The boosting won the tie with R$^2$ 0.206 against 0.183. Its settings are learning rate 0.03, depth 3, at least 50 rows per leaf and 300 rounds. The test numbers of the other models were computed after this choice and appear only in Figure~\ref{fig:models}.

Predicted points are summed per entry and sorted into a scoreboard. Our main measure is the predicted place of the real winner, because the training years hold only eleven winners, too few to learn \emph{winner or not} directly. A random order puts the winner in the middle of the field on average. For the whole scoreboard we also give the rank error, the mean distance between predicted and real place \citep{adamska2025}, against a random order drawn 1000 times per final. Two checks follow. The forest with all features is refitted without one group of features at a time on the validation finals. And every one of the 18 finals is left out once and predicted by the chosen model trained on all the others. That training includes later years, whose earlier votes already contain the year being predicted. When we tried the same check trained on earlier years only, it gave almost the same places for 2021 to 2026.

\section{Results}

\input{fig-models}

On the validation finals (Figure~\ref{fig:models}, left) the trees put the winners at or near the top, while lasso (places 5, 6 and 5) and linear regression (4, 9 and 12) do not. On the test finals the chosen boosting puts Switzerland first, Austria third and Bulgaria third, on average place 2.3 against 13.2 for a random order. The forest with all features gets 1, 2 and 8, lasso 5, 8 and 6. With three finals one surprise moves these averages a lot. On the whole scoreboard the boosting is 6.42 places off against 8.43 for a random order (95 percent range 7.18 to 9.61) and R$^2$ on the rows is 0.156. Lasso and linear regression do slightly better there (5.88 and 5.93 places), so the trees win at the top of the scoreboard and not on the whole of it. The slides of 15 October showed 1, 4 and 4 for the test winners, which were the numbers before Rest of the World was counted correctly.

\input{fig-yearly}

Leaving out every final once (Figure~\ref{fig:yearly}) puts the winner first in 4 of 18 finals and in the top three in 9, on average at place 5.7 against 13.3 for a random order. The twelve training years alone average place 7.7 with three winners in the top three. The worst misses are Germany 2010 (the year without search data) and Portugal 2017, both 16th, then Denmark 2013 (13th) and Sweden 2015 (12th). The finals from 2021 on look best, partly because the search data are better and partly because the settings were chosen on 2021 to 2023. We cannot separate the two.

\input{fig-ablation}

Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} shows what finds the winner. With all features the forest puts the validation winners at 1, 2 and 1. Without the searches they fall to 2, 9 and 18, without the earlier votes between the two countries to 2, 7 and 1 and without the running order to 7, 1 and 1. Without age and sex one winner drops one place, without lyrics nothing changes. The years 2008 to 2015 help despite their weak search data: trained on 2016 to 2019 only, the forest puts the same winners at 7, 12 and 9. How much of the earlier votes is neighbourhood is a question of its own that we leave aside here.

Our pipeline also predicts jury and public separately for 2016 to 2026, leaving out one year at a time and training on the rest. With pair features and searches the jury ranking is 6.40 places off and the public ranking 5.56, against 8.51 and 8.43 for a random order. Without searches it is 6.77 and 6.41. Searches help the public about twice as much as the jury. The two rankings correlate with Spearman 0.43 only. Computing the earlier votes from the training years alone changes these numbers by at most 0.13 places. The numbers are not directly comparable with Figure~\ref{fig:models}: the check differs and the feature set was chosen on the same years. For orientation, \citet{adamska2025} also leave out one year at a time from 2008 to 2024 and reach 6.07 places in the final with audio, lyrics, YouTube views, running order and voting history, 6.05 with YouTube views alone and 7.59 with running order and voting history, which is closest to our features, against 8.55 for chance. We do not report win probabilities. In 2000 simulations per final drawn from the errors of the public model, eight entries got a win probability above 50 percent and only one of them won.

\section{Limits}

Three test finals are few and differences below one place are within chance. Three validation finals are also few: two models found all three winners and one of them had to be picked by R$^2$, so the choice partly rests on luck. The winner's place became our main measure only after the first results were in. Before that we used the rank error. The test finals were looked at more than once along the way, with earlier versions of the models and features. The final choice was made on the validation finals, but the test set was no longer untouched. On the whole scoreboard the chosen model is not the best one. After we found that Rest of the World counted twice, we fixed it and ran the whole notebook again, including the choice of settings. Search data before 2016 are mostly empty and age and sex are missing for bands. Averaging jury and public blurs two votes that agree only loosely. People on prediction markets would want win probabilities, which we do not trust enough to report. Code and data live in a private repository of the group and the course staff can get access on request.

\section{Communication and reflection}

The spotlight presentation on 15 October explained the prediction to the target group. A blog post by Anna-Ilona follows with the submission. What our audience gets from it is a sense of how far a forecast from the day before can be trusted and which data matter for it.

\input{tab-changes}

Table~\ref{tab:changes} compares our canvas of 14 September with what we did. What worked was the pair as the unit, splitting by year, searches per country, a pipeline that runs on every machine of the group and one CSV file that let all three of us work without it. What did not work: lyrics add nothing, because a value that is the same for every voter of an entry lets the trees learn the entries by heart. The first Trends download asked for several terms at once and came back almost all zero, so each entry was queried again on 4 October. Scraping eurovisionworld with a browser failed on a teammate's laptop, which is why the pipeline has downloaded the data file behind the page since 12 October. The pipeline was hard for the others to follow, which is why the CSV file exists. Age and sex give no reliable gain and the win probabilities were badly calibrated.

We met in the workshops and wrote in a group chat. The TA approved the idea on 10 September. Data sit in one repository and the pipeline in another that reads the data at a fixed commit. Changes went in as pull requests. On 13 October it was unclear for a day whether the prediction or the neighbourhood question was our topic. On 14 October we settled on the prediction and split the work. Looking back we would decide on the measure before seeing any results, since the whole scoreboard and the winner tell different stories. We would keep the test finals closed until the end, share one simple file early, query Trends one entry at a time from the start and write the main question down in the first week. Our final slide named the next steps we see for the prediction: a neural network, the video clips themselves, better lyric sentiment and more search sources. To these we add cutting the searches two days before the final as Anna-Ilona suggested, a model that learns rankings directly and uncertainty by resampling years. Yauheni asked for a window that ends after the second semi-final, which the current one already does.

We learned that a random split leaks when rows belong together and that a measurement taken after the event is no forecast. A source has to be understood before it is queried, as Trends showed with its shared scale. Features that are the same for a whole entry tempt trees to memorise. Reproducible means that it runs on every machine in the group and not only on the one where the code was written. And the chosen measure decides how good a result looks: 6.42 places off and a winner in first, third and third place describe the same model.
