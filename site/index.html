Song or neighbours?

Anna-Ilona Nikolaeva, Yauheni Zviazdou and Tim Kicker
Introduction to Data Science, University of Helsinki, October 2026

Since 2016 Cyprus has made it to the Eurovision final eight times. Every single time, the Greek public gave it twelve points1. Was that the song or was that Greece?

We went through every vote in the ten finals from 2016 to 2026 and kept the jury and the public apart. For each vote we asked how much of it the song explains and how much the relationship between the two countries.

Juries and the public disagree

Each dot is one song in a final. The further a dot sits from the line, the more the juries and the public disagreed about it. Israel in 2024 got 52 points from the juries and 323 from the public. Switzerland in 2025 got 214 from the juries and none at all from the public.

The neighbours matter more to the public

To measure the song we look at how well it did with everybody else. To measure the relationship we look at how close the two countries are (distance, a shared border or language) and how they voted for each other in earlier years.

Knowing only the song, we can predict 40 percent of the differences in what the public gives. Knowing only the two countries adds 12 percent. For the juries the same numbers are 25 and 3 percent, so the neighbours count about four times as much for the public as they do for the juries.

Who favours whom

For every pair of countries we compare the points one gave the other with what the song got from everybody else. The difference, averaged over all finals the two countries shared, shows who favours whom. We only list pairs that met in at least three finals.

          Who this is for

          If you follow Eurovision, you can check the old line about neighbours with real numbers and see which countries your own country favours. If you work for a delegation, you can see which part of a result you can change with the song and its promotion and which part comes from neighbours and diaspora. Juries and the public also react differently, so the same entry can need a different plan for each.

          How we did it

          We put the results of every final since 1957 next to what connects each pair of countries (distance, a shared border or language). A model learned from nine finals at a time and was always tested on the tenth. The technical report has the details.
